Welford's Algorithm & Sampling Distributions

Deriving one-pass variance formulas and analyzing sampling distributions across multiple trajectories.

When you first write a script to calculate variance, the instinct is to use the standard textbook formula: calculate the sum of the squares, subtract the square of the sum, and divide by $n$. It works perfectly on a whiteboard. But if you run that in JavaScript over millions of data points, you’ll quickly run into catastrophic cancellation. Floating-point precision completely falls apart when subtracting two massive numbers.

To fix this, we need a one-pass algorithm that updates the mean and the sum of squared errors (SS) incrementally.

Part 1: Deriving Welford’s Recurrence

Let $M_n$ be the sum of squared differences from the mean for $n$ samples: $M_n = \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x}n)^2$. We want to express $M_n$ in terms of $M{n-1}$ and the new observation $x_n$.

First, let’s look at the residual of the new observation relative to the old mean. We know Knuth’s formula for the mean updates like this: \(\bar{x}_n = \bar{x}_{n-1} + \frac{x_n - \bar{x}_{n-1}}{n}\)

By rearranging, we can express the difference between the new mean and the old mean: \(\bar{x}_n - \bar{x}_{n-1} = \frac{x_n - \bar{x}_{n-1}}{n}\)

Now, let’s expand the definition of $M_n$: \(M_n = \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x}_n)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} (x_i - \bar{x}_n)^2 + (x_n - \bar{x}_n)^2\)

We can rewrite the term inside the summation by introducing the old mean $\bar{x}_{n-1}$: \(x_i - \bar{x}_n = (x_i - \bar{x}_{n-1}) - (\bar{x}_n - \bar{x}_{n-1})\)

Plugging this back into the sum and expanding the square: \(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \left[ (x_i - \bar{x}_{n-1}) - (\bar{x}_n - \bar{x}_{n-1}) \right]^2\) \(= \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} (x_i - \bar{x}_{n-1})^2 - 2(\bar{x}_n - \bar{x}_{n-1})\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(x_i - \bar{x}_{n-1}) + (n-1)(\bar{x}_n - \bar{x}_{n-1})^2\)

Notice that $\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(x_i - \bar{x}{n-1}) = 0$, because the sum of deviations from the mean is always zero. This kills the middle term. The first term is simply our previous sum of squares, $M{n-1}$. So we are left with: \(M_n = M_{n-1} + (n-1)(\bar{x}_n - \bar{x}_{n-1})^2 + (x_n - \bar{x}_n)^2\)

Substitute $(\bar{x}n - \bar{x}{n-1}) = \frac{x_n - \bar{x}_{n-1}}{n}$ into the equation: \(M_n = M_{n-1} + (n-1)\left(\frac{x_n - \bar{x}_{n-1}}{n}\right)^2 + (x_n - \bar{x}_n)^2\)

Since $(x_n - \bar{x}n) = \frac{n-1}{n}(x_n - \bar{x}{n-1})$, you can factor out the terms. After a bit of algebraic cleanup, it collapses beautifully into Welford’s recurrence formula: \(M_n = M_{n-1} + (x_n - \bar{x}_{n-1})(x_n - \bar{x}_n)\)

Statistical InterpretationComparing the simulated outputs with the theoretical targets highlights exactly why inferential statistics works.First, regardless of how small $n$ is, the average of the sampling means sits right on top of the theoretical $\mu = 5$. The same happens for the variances: the average of the sampling variances tightly converges to $\sigma^2 \approx 8.333$. This proves practically that the sample mean and the sample variance (using $n-1$, Bessel’s correction) are unbiased estimators. If you run the simulation, you’ll see that dividing the SS by $n$ instead of $n-1$ consistently underestimates the true variance, which is exactly why the correction exists.The real insight comes from moving the $n$ slider (the number of systems each hacker attempts). When $n$ is small (e.g., $n=5$), the distributions are wide. A hacker might get “lucky” and score an average of 8, or “unlucky” and score an average of 2. However, as you increase $n$, the variability of both the sampling means and sampling variances collapses. The histograms narrow into sharp spikes. The standard error of the mean shrinks proportionally to $\sigma / \sqrt{n}$. In a security assessment context, this proves that evaluating a hacker’s capability based on a few attempts is statistically unreliable due to high sample variance, but expanding the penetration test across hundreds of systems eliminates the noise and reveals their true expected capability.
